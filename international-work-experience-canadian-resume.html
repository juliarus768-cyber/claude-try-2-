How to Adapt International Work Experience for a Canadian Resume

Practical guidance for professionals presenting overseas experience to Canadian employers.

By Julia Cher, CRS · Published 2026-10-20

Julia Cher holds the Certified Career Practitioner and Certified Resume Strategist (CRS) credentials from Career Professionals of Canada (CPC).

International work experience belongs on a Canadian resume when it helps explain your fit for the role. The editing task is to make that experience understandable: what the organization did, what you were responsible for and what evidence you can offer. Changing countries does not make previous work irrelevant.

Start with one realistic target. A resume for an administrative coordinator should emphasize different evidence from a resume for a purchasing assistant, even when both draw on the same employment history. Our Canadian resume guide for newcomers explains the broader adaptation process; this article works through the experience section.

Help the reader understand the context

A recruiter may recognize the work but not your employer's name, local terminology or reporting structure. Give enough context to make the responsibilities clear. Avoid assuming that every Canadian employer interprets international experience in the same way, or that adding a Canadian-sounding title will resolve a genuine qualification gap.

For an unfamiliar employer, add a short description immediately below its name or within the role heading. A useful description identifies the organization’s activity. It does not need to advertise the company or reproduce its history.

Fictional employer-description example

All examples in this article are fictional editing exercises, not client cases or results. Use only details that are true of your own work.

Less clear: Northbridge Distribution, Warsaw, Poland.

More informative: Northbridge Distribution, Warsaw, Poland — regional distributor of packaged food to independent retailers.

The second version explains the business. Do not add “market leader,” a revenue figure or a customer count unless you have reliable evidence and the detail helps the application.

Build a posting-to-evidence worksheet before editing

Read the target posting for responsibilities, required qualifications and tools. Separate essential requirements from preferences. Then write down evidence from your own work for the requirements you meet. Job Bank also recommends tailoring a resume to the role and presenting accomplishments honestly in its resume-writing guidance.

  1. Posting requirement: coordinate appointments and maintain records.
    Your evidence: scheduled customer visits, updated a shared calendar and recorded changes in a customer database.
    Editing decision: show scheduling and record accuracy together, using the actual tool name if relevant.
  2. Posting requirement: resolve order enquiries.
    Your evidence: checked delivery status and worked with warehouse colleagues to answer customer questions.
    Editing decision: name the customer issue and your contribution instead of writing only “customer service.”
  3. Posting requirement: use a particular accounting application.
    Your evidence: experience with a different system.
    Editing decision: name the system you used. Explain relevant processes, but do not list the requested application as a skill you already have.

This fictional worksheet is a way to test relevance, not a list of bullets to copy. If you cannot explain an example in an interview, leave it out or clarify what you actually did. For formatting and accurate keyword use, see our ATS resume tips for Canadian job seekers.

Replace broad duties with specific contributions

A responsibility becomes easier to assess when it names the action, the work involved and the people or process affected. Start with those details. Add a result only when you can support it. A useful bullet does not require a percentage.

Fictional before-and-after examples

Before: Responsible for documentation.

After: Checked customer order forms for missing information and followed up with the sales team before orders were passed to dispatch.

Before: Worked with different departments.

After: Collected stock updates from warehouse colleagues and shared revised delivery information with customer service staff.

Before: Improved reports by 40%.

After, when no reliable measure exists: Created a weekly order-status summary so colleagues could identify outstanding customer follow-ups.

The last revision removes an unsupported number. It is appropriate only if creating that summary was part of the person's real work; it does not justify replacing one invented claim with another.

Keep the distinction between ownership and participation. “Prepared information for the purchasing manager” is different from “approved supplier contracts.” Use “supported,” “coordinated,” “prepared” or “approved” according to your actual authority.

Show transferable skills through the work

Transferable skills are more persuasive when attached to a task. Instead of listing “communication, organization and teamwork” without context, describe how you explained an order change, maintained a schedule or coordinated a handover.

A professional changing industries might have relevant scheduling, documentation or stakeholder communication experience while still needing industry training. Present both points honestly. A shared skill does not automatically establish eligibility for a technical or regulated role.

  • Communication: who needed the information, and what did you explain?
  • Organization: what records, schedules or competing tasks did you maintain?
  • Problem-solving: what issue did you investigate, and what action was yours?
  • Leadership: did you supervise people, train colleagues or coordinate tasks without line-management authority?

Keep employers, dates, titles and credentials accurate

Retain the actual employer, work location and dates. Use an accurate English rendering of your title, with a short functional explanation if necessary. Our companion article explains how to explain foreign job titles without overstating seniority.

List education and professional credentials accurately. If you have a completed credential assessment, describe what its report says rather than assigning your own Canadian equivalency. If a role requires licensing or registration, check the relevant authority's requirements; resume wording does not replace that process.

Do not conceal an employment gap by changing dates. If a gap needs context, use a brief, truthful explanation. See how to explain employment gaps on a Canadian resume for examples that avoid oversharing.

Review the whole experience entry

Read the employer description, title and bullets together. A factual descriptor can make a job understandable without adding another sentence to every bullet. Remove repeated duties and give the most relevant work more space. Older or less relevant work can be shorter without changing its dates or meaning.

  • Can a reader understand what the organization did?
  • Does each important bullet connect to a real requirement of the target role?
  • Are unfamiliar acronyms explained on first use?
  • Do the verbs reflect your authority and contribution?
  • Can you support every metric, credential and tool claim?
  • Does the wording still sound like something you could explain naturally in an interview?

Avoid removing all overseas detail, copying whole sentences from a posting, translating every responsibility literally or replacing a specialized title with a more senior local one. The goal is clarity, not a different employment history.

When a second perspective may help

If you have a clear target but cannot decide which experience to emphasize, professional resume assistance may help organize the evidence and prepare targeted documents. If you are still choosing a direction, settle that question before treating wording as the only problem. You may also be able to make the changes yourself using the worksheet above.
